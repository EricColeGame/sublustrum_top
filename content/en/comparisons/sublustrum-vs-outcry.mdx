## Two Titles, One Game: How the Naming Split Happened

In the late 2000s, small European studios frequently shipped a game in their home market first and then licensed it to a Western publisher for English-speaking territories. Renaming was routine, because a title that reads beautifully in one language can land flatly in another — and marketing departments rarely trust a word most players cannot pronounce.

The game is commonly credited in English-language databases to Russian developer Phantomery Interactive, a small studio whose work sits firmly in the atmospheric, first-person adventure tradition rather than the mainstream point-and-click mold.

### The Home-Market Title

Sublustrum is the original name, and it fits the material unusually well. It carries a sense of dim light, dusk, and things half-seen — an accurate description of a game built on ambiguity, empty corridors, and environments that feel like memories rather than locations. Players who encountered the game first in Russian tend to use this name out of habit, and it is the term you will see in older forum threads and regional retail listings.

### The International Title

Outcry is the name English-speaking audiences were given. It is shorter, harsher, and easier to say aloud in a trailer voice-over, which is exactly why publishers favored it. The trade-off is that it tells you almost nothing about the game's actual tone, which is quiet and uneasy rather than loud. That mismatch is one reason newcomers occasionally assume Outcry is a horror-action title rather than a contemplative adventure.

### What Actually Changes Between the Two Releases

- **Language and voice work.** The Russian edition keeps the original performances; the international edition replaces them with an English dub. Player experience tends to split here, with some preferring the original delivery and others grateful for a dub they can follow without subtitles.
- **Subtitles and menus.** Localized releases sometimes add extra subtitle languages, which makes them easier to recommend to players outside the two primary markets.
- **Packaging and marketing.** Different cover art, different blurbs, and different age-rating logos. None of this touches the game itself.
- **Technical patches.** Compatibility fixes were handled separately by region, so one edition may run more smoothly out of the box than the other.
- **Price and scarcity.** Because the two names surface in different listings, the same game can carry wildly different asking prices depending on which word the seller used.

## Frequently Asked Questions About Sublustrum vs Outcry

**Are Sublustrum and Outcry the same game?**
Yes. Community reports and database entries consistently describe them as one title released under two names — a Russian-language edition and an international edition. There is no separate sequel, remake, or spin-off hiding behind either word.

**Which one should a first-time player choose?**
If English is your first language, Outcry is usually the smoother introduction because the interface, subtitles, and voice work match the language of most available walkthroughs. If you understand Russian and care about original performances, Sublustrum is the more authentic option.

**Is Outcry a remake or an updated version of Sublustrum?**
No. It is not a remake and it is not a remaster. The underlying game, including its puzzles and structure, is the same; what changes is localization, packaging, and regional publishing.

**Why does the Sublustrum vs Outcry debate keep coming back?**
Because search engines, resale listings, and game databases treat the two names as separate entries. That split creates duplicate listings, mismatched prices, and the persistent impression that two games exist. Once you know it is one title under two labels, the confusion disappears — and you can spend your time playing instead of comparing store pages.
