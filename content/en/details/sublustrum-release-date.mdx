## Why the Sublustrum Release Date Is Harder to Pin Down Than You'd Expect

If you've gone looking for the **Sublustrum release date**, you've probably noticed something frustrating: different sources give you slightly different answers, and none of them explains why. That matters more than it sounds. Release timing determines which build of the game you're actually looking at, whether a patch is already baked in, and whether the copy you're eyeing is an original launch version or a later repackaged release.

This guide breaks down what's reliably known about the **Sublustrum release date**, where the confusion comes from, and how to confirm the details yourself in a few minutes.

Sublustrum is an atmospheric, puzzle-driven adventure game from a small Czech development team. Like many independent releases from its era, it never had a single, globally synchronized launch day that everyone could point to afterward.

What exists instead is a cluster of dates that each describe something slightly different:

- The day the game first became publicly available in its home region
- The day an English-language build appeared
- The day a digital storefront added a product page
- The day a patch, update, or re-release went live

Any one of those can end up labeled "the release date" depending on who's writing. That's the root of nearly every conflicting answer you'll find.

## Sublustrum Release Timeline: What the Records Actually Show

The honest summary is that the original release is generally placed in the early 2010s, with one year repeated more often than any other in databases and retrospectives. Treat that year as an approximation, not a verified stamp.

| Milestone | Most commonly cited timing | Confidence level |
|---|---|---|
| Original PC release (home region) | Early 2010s; one year repeated most often | Moderate |
| English-language availability | Shortly after the original; timing varies by source | Low to moderate |
| Digital storefront product pages | Added later, often years after launch | High — as listing dates only |

| Community wiki and forum entries | Inconsistent; often inherit earlier errors | Low |

The pattern here is worth internalizing: **confidence drops the further you get from the original launch**. Primary sources are scarce, and secondary sources tend to copy each other rather than verify independently.

### The Listing Date Trap

The single most common mistake people make is reading a store page's date and assuming it's the launch date. A product page date tells you when that page went live on that storefront — which, for older indie titles, can be years after the game was first playable.

If a listing says a date in the mid-to-late 2010s, that almost certainly reflects a digital re-release or a newly created storefront entry, not the game's debut.

## Platforms and Regions: Why the Date Keeps Moving

Sublustrum was built for Windows PC, and that's the platform virtually every reference agrees on. Everything beyond that is where dates start to drift.

| Context | What the date actually refers to | Practical takeaway |
|---|---|---|
| Original Windows release | First public availability | Closest thing to a true launch date |
| Regional storefront page | Local publish or availability date | Frequently later than launch |
| Digital re-release | Date a build was republished | Often mistaken for the original |
| Community archive upload | Date a file was uploaded | Never a release date |
| Review or retrospective | Date the writer was given | Secondary and unverified |

Regional differences matter more than most people expect. A game that appeared in its home market first could take months — or considerably longer — to show up in other territories, especially when translation, distribution, and storefront approval were handled by a very small team.

That gap is exactly why you'll see the same game described as launching in two different years by two sources that are both technically correct about their own region.

## How to Verify the Sublustrum Release Date Yourself

You don't need special tools. You need a consistent order of operations, because checking sources in the wrong order is how bad dates spread.

1. **Start with the developer or publisher.** If a studio page, archived announcement, or press release exists, it outranks everything else.
2. **Check the store listing, but read it carefully.** Note whether the date is labeled as a release date or just a page creation date.
3. **Cross-reference a games database.** MobyGames, IGDB, and similar catalogs are curated and usually cite their sources.
4. **Look at the citation, not the claim.** A database entry with a primary source beats one with none.
5. **Use community sources last.** Wikis and forums are useful for corroboration, never as a starting point.

| Source type | Strength | Weakness |
|---|---|---|
| Developer / publisher page | Primary and authoritative | Often offline for older indie titles |
| Store product page | Official, region-specific | Date may reflect the listing, not launch |
| Games database | Curated, sourced | Occasionally copies other databases |
| Encyclopedia entries | Aggregated and cited | Only as good as the citation |
| Forums and wikis | Firsthand player reports | Unverified, contradictory |

If you want to see what's currently available, start with a search rather than a product page — you can [look up Sublustrum through Steam's store search](https://store.steampowered.com/search/?term=Sublustrum) and check whether a listing exists in your region, then cross-reference any date you find against a games database.

That context explains a lot about why the game still gets discussed. It isn't remembered for marketing reach — it's remembered because the atmosphere stuck with people who played it.
